## The four elements

PULL is an acronym. All four have to be true at once, for one real person, right now.

| | Element | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | **Project** | A project on the potential customer's to-do list that is being prioritised *now*. |
| **U** | **Unavoidable** | A reason this project is urgent now, versus everything else on an infinite backlog. They are in a situation where they would be weird *not* to prioritise it. |
| **L** | **List** | The options they consider for getting it done. Not just your competitors — technologies, hiring, spreadsheets, doing nothing. |
| **L** | **Limitations** | Those options have limitations severe enough to block the progress they are trying to make. |

The test Snyder keeps returning to: a person with PULL **would be weird not to buy** your product, given their priorities and their options. Everyone else would be weird *to* buy it.

I consider Squarespace, Framer and traditional website builders, but they have a big learning curve for making anything actually look how I want it to look.](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/35b71cdd-ddfa-4c14-93a0-fe3fea215fa8_607x672.png)

## The base assumption

Start from indifference. People do not care about you, your product, or how you think the world should work — and they do not want to be convinced they should. Total indifference is the default state for most people, most of the time.

People care about themselves: their jobs, their lives, the things they are trying to get done. Occasionally what you have built fits what they are already trying to do. That fit is the only reliable exit from indifference.

## PULL as blocked demand

The quickest way in is to pair the letters:

- **P + U** — a person applying force against their status quo, trying to accomplish something.
- **L + L** — their existing options are not good enough, pushing back in favour of that status quo.

Or in one line: **an unavoidable project meets unworkable options.**

P and U are *demand*. The two Ls are *supply options blocking that demand*. So PULL is a state of **blocked demand** — and your product is what unblocks it.

![A Trello board of projects, annotated: someone with budget is actively trying to accomplish something on their to-do list, but their existing options are not good enough — they are struggling or blocked, and you can unblock them.](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/2d013412-ef1b-4b48-b323-c093da5727af_1203x408.png)

## Why not "problems" and "pain points"

You could call blocked demand a "problem" and call the bad-fit options "pain points". Snyder deliberately avoids both words, because the categories barely overlap:

- Most **problems** are not blocked demand. Climate change is a problem; it is not obviously demand.
- Most **blocked demand** is not a problem. Buying someone a gift involves no problem at all.
- Most **pain points** — including hair-on-fire ones — we simply live with.

![The Venn Diagram of Suffering: two ellipses labelled Problems and Stuff we take action on, touching at only a very small sliver of overlap.](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/f6e14801-c8a2-4481-ad87-177fcddd67e7_983x487.png)

He calls this the **Venn Diagram of Suffering**, and it is the trap it names: you go and find big problems and severe pain points, and then spend years building something nobody buys.

## Demand is not desire for supply

"Make something people want" sounds right until you ask whether anyone has ever *wanted* an ERP, a CRM, or a data visualisation tool. Nobody has. Yet those products are enormous, and Dreamforce is a conference that adults attend voluntarily.

The paradox dissolves once you separate the two sides:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Supply** | What we build. |
| **Demand** | What a person is trying to achieve in their life. |

**Demand is supply-agnostic.** Nobody's demand is "buy a CRM" — that is a supply category. The demand is something like *organise my sales team's data so I can manage and forecast effectively*, and a CRM is one option on the list.

When the demand side is strong enough — urgent project, bad-fit options — people behave *as if* they want your product. They pull it out of your hands, thank you for a janky MVP, and show up to your user conference.

## What causes PULL: the situation

Underneath PULL sits a **situation** that makes one specific project urgent or unavoidable, forcing it above everything else on the backlog. A person's situation shapes their to-do list.

Many people *could* be prioritising a given project right now. You want the situations that make it weird not to be.

## PULL defines the shape of supply

Watch a project move from backlog, to #1 priority, to something shaped and detailed — and then get blocked. The blocking is what carves out a specific, defined space your product has to fit in order to be pulled.

![The Physics of PULL, another visualization: a backlog of 500 projects, one of which becomes the number one priority when something happens; the project is then shaped with specifics and paths A and B are ruled out by limitations X and Y; leaving a dashed gap labelled "something that helps us accomplish this project without X or Y" — a space for supply to fit.](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/bf3b21cf-649c-49f9-a855-bee9126f25f0_1042x572.png)

PULL also fixes the smaller supply details: the pricing buyers expect, the onboarding they expect, and what they need to accomplish before they will renew.

## Who has PULL is your ICP

Defined properly, PULL describes someone who would be weird not to buy. Everyone else is prioritising a different project, or their existing options are already good enough.

People with PULL sit at the centre of the bullseye. They buy without convincing, because your product unblocks what they are already doing: roughly 100% closed-won, roughly 100% post-sale success, fast cycles where **they** supply most of the effort to get the deal done.

![A bullseye diagram: the black centre is ICP with PULL, weird not to buy, giving roughly 100% win rates, fast predictable sales cycles and roughly 100% retention post-sale. The surrounding rings are "could buy" and "also could buy" — people outside the bullseye can buy, but slower, less predictably and with lower win rates.](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/ed0e92ac-543a-4b3a-b5e0-99ca0a49751e_1051x462.png)

People outside the bullseye *can* buy. It just takes more convincing and pushing, converts worse, succeeds less often post-sale, and moves slower — and they arrive with random feature requests and don't "see the value". Focusing on them is opportunity cost.

## PULL at scale: rivers, waves, dams

Demand exists only at the level of an individual buyer and their mental to-do list. Companies do not have demand, because a company cannot buy anything. Neither do groups, personas or niches.

Zoom out and multiple people are prioritising the same project at any one moment — but most of them have good-enough options, so they have the project without the PULL. The subset who are both prioritising *and* blocked is your market at t=now, and it has a shape:

| Shape | PULL over time | Startup approach |
|---|---|---|
| **Rivers** | Flat and constant | Find the subset with the most PULL and serve them best, though you often need feature parity to compete. |
| **Waves** | Small today, big later | Tinker at the frontier, build for people already there, and wait for the wave to hit as the market scales. |
| **Dams** | Massive today, bursting | Test demand by selling first, then build the right product and scale as fast as humanly possible. Dams are rare. |

![How Demand Works At Scale: waves, rivers and dams. Demand exists only at the level of the individual buyer; companies, groups, personas and niches cannot buy anything. Diagrams show people prioritising a project across past, present and future, then the subset whose existing options are good enough versus those who actually have PULL, and finally the three demand shapes over time.](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/b172d310-2c60-4a90-ad90-40c84afc94e3_845x937.png)

## Worked example and attack vectors

Snyder writes up his own PULL for the founders he advises, colour-coding the four elements so the parts are visible:

![A colour-coded example PULL writeup. The project is getting unstuck and moving faster on the path to $1M+ ARR, repeatable sales, PMF and insanely fast growth. The situation is feeling that they should be further along: sales calls not converting enough, struggling to schedule meetings, unsure which segment to double down on. The list of options is DIY and a traditional sales coach. The limitations are that DIY is not fast enough and a traditional sales coach lacks founder experience and may not get the complexity of 0-1. A key maps each colour to project, situation, list of options, and limitations.](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/054b38e0-deb3-496e-a269-805b4431d87e_1380x432.png)

Three things he points out about his own writeup:

- **The situation is the real driver.** Something in their world is making them think *we should be further along*.
- **The project is deliberately abstract** compared to something concrete like Fletch's "fix your confusing website messaging". A more productised version of his own would be closer to "fix your sales calls that aren't converting".
- **The whole thing is shaped to cohere.** The project is worded to fit against the limitations of a traditional sales coach, and the phrase "get unstuck and move faster" is chosen to select away from people who will DIY it.

He calls each viable permutation an **attack vector** — more concrete, more abstract, more aspirational — and each one generates a different ICP, a different ideal supply shape, and ultimately a different business. Choosing among them is, by his own account, unsolved.

## The implementation hierarchy

The Quickstart's core claim: PULL operates in a strict hierarchical order, so **there are many ways to be right downstream that are counterproductive because you are wrong upstream.** Work top down.

### 1. ICP = destiny

Everything is downstream of how you define your ICP. Define it as *who has PULL right now* — not who you want to sell to, and not even who would benefit most.

Two sanity checks on a filled-out framework:

1. Is it OK if this person doesn't do this project right now?
2. Assuming they do it, are they OK not buying from you and doing it some other way?

Both should be uncomfortable to answer yes to. They are only sanity checks, though — the real test is how someone you *think* has PULL behaves in a sales conversation. Base the framework on one real person, or it is nearly impossible to get right. That person should buy a junky v1 with near-zero convincing, and push you to improve it rather than churning. It will always be a subset of who you eventually want to serve, and that is fine.

### 2. Product

The most upstream product requirement falls straight out of the framework: **your product must let them overcome the limitations so they can accomplish the project.** Other requirements exist, and they will surface on their own as objections while selling and frustrations while delivering.

*How* you enable that is design work. Architecture for future optionality matters eventually, but never at the expense of fitting PULL. An MVP can be a service — a "Ratatouille" version with a human behind it — as long as it confirms the PULL is real.

### 3. Sales, the vibe

Real PULL buys whatever fits, provided you don't talk them out of it. They will spend their own energy to purchase, because you are unblocking something that matters. If you don't see that energy, the PULL isn't real and the interesting question is why.

Without PULL, even a career top rep struggles. So: chill out. Don't build the legal case, don't show every feature, don't insist your vision is infallible. Check whether they need help, and don't be bothered when they don't.

### 4. Getting people to talk to you

If someone has PULL it mostly doesn't matter how you reach them. If they don't, that also doesn't matter.

- **Before** you know what PULL is, a cold product pitch has roughly zero chance. Pitch something interesting instead — use your personal monopoly, the thing only you can say that is worth their time.
- **After** you know it, you know their situation, so you know where to find them in it. Sometimes that's cold calls or events; often it's something creative that only makes sense for this specific PULL. He calls that a **toll booth**.

Target 5–15+ conversations a week. If you can't get people to talk to you, nothing else matters.

### 5. Discovery is filling out their PULL framework

The opening sequence that earns the right to ask questions:

1. *Why did you take the call? What would be most helpful to get out of it?*
2. Set the agenda, confirm it's what they want.
3. *Before we get to that, mind if I ask a few questions to understand your context?*

Then ask questions that surface PULL — not general context, not pain points. Play it back when you think you have it, because you often don't. And make it comfortable for them to admit they don't really have PULL, rather than interrogating them or being over-enthusiastic.

### 6. Pitch and demo

Describe the product with the smallest possible description, using the supply template:

> We are [product category] that enables [people like ICP] to do [project] without [limitations].

Then explain how it works by describing their unblocked life — *instead of XYZ, you now do ABC and get DEF* — rather than how the software literally works. Both together: 60–90 seconds, one or two simple visuals.

Leave out your theory of the world and the big problem you are solving. The more you show that isn't tied to their PULL, the more likely you are to convince them you're irrelevant.

Then stop for a **fit check**: does this conceptually fit what you're looking for? Make no and *I'm not sure* easy answers. If it isn't a no, ask what they'd need to know to decide — that either closes on the call or sets the next agenda. Demos are optional, three minutes maximum, minimum screens. Never show setup. Never show a settings page.

### 7. Deal strategy

Optimise early deals for **velocity**, usually starting small to minimise the surface area of objections and approvals. The reason is learning rate: you only find out whether PULL is real with volume all the way through the system, and you only truly know when someone buys *and* commits to renew.

Long-term pricing is a function of the value of getting the project done versus the cost of the alternatives on their list. Early on, don't give it away — you won't learn whether PULL is real — but don't maximise either. The target: enough that they'll shout at you rather than ghost you if it doesn't deliver, not so much that it kills velocity. Selling services first is a legitimate way to not run out of cash.

### 8. Sales process

Fit is established on call one, maybe spilling into call two. After that it is project management.

- Open every later call by recapping their PULL and what they wanted to assess.
- The person with PULL is your champion; you are helping them finish their project.
- Work backwards from when they want it done and define the minimum steps to purchase.
- Group demos are you helping your champion convince their peers, not you convincing the room. Ask to be in those meetings — it helps them, so it isn't pushy.

### 9. Post-sales

Retention and expansion are the real test of PMF. Don't pour fuel on acquisition until retention is consistent.

New customers **churn by default** until something changes that. That something is occasionally "product usage" but is usually more specific and slightly weird. Find it, find the leading indicator of it, then turn onboarding into a bullet train to that indicator — to the point where it would be weird for a new customer not to hit it. Churn is unpleasant and is also the best available signal about what PULL really is. Lean into it.

## Common errors

- **Debugging at the wrong level.** A urgency problem in your sales process can be patched at that level, or dissolved by defining an ICP where urgency isn't an issue. Problems felt at one level are almost always best solved higher up.
- **Treating companies as having PULL.** Only individuals do, and two people at the same company often have opposite PULL. Senior people tend to have clearer PULL and the ability to buy; an excited junior champion frequently gets stiff-armed on a $50/month purchase.
- **Not having found PULL and hoping it resolves itself.** It doesn't. Go find someone who has it.
- **Grinding through calls against a broken hypothesis.** If 3–5 calls with people who fit your hypothesis produce no pulling, the hypothesis is wrong. Don't run hundreds.
- **Doing "research" instead of the job.** Go do the work alongside a potential customer, or ask the Hail Mary question: *what would you pay me to solve for you?* Finding PULL purely by interviewing people would be strange.
- **Too few sales calls.** If you're doing outbound, it's almost certainly messaging. Some niches just require picking up the phone.
- **Some buy fast, some buy slow.** A sign you don't yet understand PULL. Deal velocity is time-until-PULL plus the actual cycle; variable lengths mean you don't know when PULL happens or what it is.

## Appendix: signals you are in the not(PMF) zone

We know what PMF looks like — customers buy with little convincing, treat the product as irresistible, pull it out of your hands — and we know what its absence looks like. What's usually missing is the cause. Snyder's answer is that startups crossing from not(PMF) to PMF figure out one thing: PULL.

Signals you're in the zone:

- Customers aren't pulling. You convince them to buy, and when that works, they buy slowly.
- You're grinding through pilots or long cycles, doing all the following up. Buying is clearly not their top priority.
- Tactics that work elsewhere — sales playbooks, outbound scripts, PLG, building more — haven't worked for you, and it isn't clear why.

The zone takes all comers, from zero revenue to tens of millions, across software, services, AI, B2B, B2C and deep tech. A business can be profitable and still be in it: sales always hard, growth always brutal, retention always arm-twisting.

Two framings worth keeping:

- **You don't need much.** One person in a PULL state, one thing they rip out of your hands. That is one "Hell Yes" case study.
- **A startup is a case study factory.** You find the one case study and build a system to repeat it. Viewed as a factory with three steps — get people with PULL talking to you, convert them, keep them — you can find the single bottleneck. The founder's only job is to attack the constraint.
